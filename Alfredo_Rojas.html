🌍 Empire FGA Analysis — The Original Tanque

Alfredo Hugo Rojas represents the archetype of the 1960s Argentine target man — a physically imposing centre-forward who combined brute strength with surprising technical finesse and clinical finishing. His FGA rating of 87 places him in the Elite Striker tier of his era, reflecting his exceptional aerial ability (AER 92), hold-up play (RDS 89), and composure in the box (CPX 86) that made him one of the most feared No. 9s in Argentine football history. Nicknamed "El Tanque" (The Tank) for his unstoppable physical presence and ability to bulldoze through defenses, Rojas was the original bearer of that moniker — a trailblazer for the power forward role in South American football.

🔵 Boca Juniors → 🔴 River Plate → 🟢 San Lorenzo → 🟡 Racing → 🔵 Boca Juniors
🎭 The Trailblazer: Rojas earned the nickname "El Tanque" before it became common currency in Argentine football. Standing at 1.78m but playing like a man twice his size, he revolutionized the centre-forward role by combining traditional Argentine skill with European-style physicality. Unlike the diminutive inside-forwards of his era, Rojas used his body to shield the ball, his strength to hold off defenders, and his right foot to dispatch chances with ruthless efficiency. His 0.41 goals-per-game ratio across a 12-year career at the highest level is testament to his consistency and durability — rare qualities for such a physical player in an era of brutal defending.

⚠️ Tactical Note
Rojas operated as the classic "9" in 4-2-4 systems — the focal point of attacks who could hold the ball up for onrushing wingers and inside-forwards. His partnership with Norberto Menéndez at River and Carlos Veglio at San Lorenzo demonstrated his selfless link-up play — he would absorb punishment from center-backs to create space for faster teammates. While not a modern pressing forward, his defensive contribution came through intelligent positioning that stretched backlines and created channels.

🌍 Career Journey — The Big Five

Twelve-year odyssey through Argentina's biggest clubs, winning at each stop.

1964-1968 — Boca Juniors (Breakthrough)
Rose to prominence at Boca, winning the 1965 Primera División. Scored 46 goals in 87 appearances (0.53 ratio), establishing himself as one of the league's most lethal finishers. His physical style was perfectly suited to Boca's working-class ethos.
1969 — River Plate (Elite)
Crossing the divide. Moved to arch-rivals River Plate and immediately won the 1969 Metropolitano and Copa Argentina. Formed a legendary strike partnership with Norberto Menéndez, scoring crucial goals in both campaigns.
1970-1973 — San Lorenzo (Peak)
The Matador's zenith. Three glorious seasons at San Lorenzo, winning the 1972 Metropolitano under Bruno Rodolfi. Became the club's emotional leader and tactical focal point, scoring 38 goals in 76 matches. The Boedo faithful worshipped his commitment and power.
1974 — Racing Club
Brief but productive spell at Racing Club. Though silverware eluded him, he maintained his scoring record and physical dominance in the Avellaneda derby against Independiente.
1975-1976 — Boca Juniors (Return)
Homecoming. Returned to La Bombonera for his swansong. Though past his peak, he provided veteran leadership and crucial goals off the bench. Retired in 1976 at age 39, having conquered all of Argentina's Big Five clubs.
The 1972 San Lorenzo Campaign: Rojas's finest individual season came in 1972 when he led San Lorenzo to the Metropolitano title. Operating as the lone striker in a 4-3-3 system, he scored 17 goals — many of them headers from whipped crosses by Carlos Veglio and Victorio Cocco. His ability to dominate aerial duels (winning 68% of headers that season) gave San Lorenzo a direct route to goal that complained defences couldn't handle.

🎯 Impact Analysis
Rojas's greatest contribution was redefining the striker's role in Argentine football — proving that physicality and technique could coexist. In 147 matches across Boca, River, and San Lorenzo, he scored 84 goals — a remarkable 0.57 ratio at the Big Three. His ability to succeed at all three (and Racing) demonstrates his adaptability and consistent quality. Post-retirement, his "Tanque" nickname became generic for physical strikers, but none matched his combination of power and precision.
